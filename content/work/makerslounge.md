## The challenge

Builders thrive when they share their work. The people building with AI in
Toronto had no shortage of ideas; what they lacked was a place to make those
ideas real by telling one person, face to face, and getting a reaction. Online
you post into a void. In a room, someone asks the question that changes the
project.

MakersLounge started as five people at a coffee shop. The job was to turn
that into a program: something that happens on a schedule, that people trust
enough to give up an evening for, and that gets better each time.

## The program

Four formats, each with a different job.

- **Meetups.** Monthly build-and-demo nights, mostly at New Stadium on Walnut
  Ave. Show what you're building, meet the people who care about the same
  problem, leave with one new person to follow up with.
- **Workshops.** Hands-on sessions where everyone leaves with something
  running: a Claude Code workshop and an AI agent-building workshop at
  Toronto Metropolitan University, and an online session on working with
  context in Claude.
- **Hackathons.** An AI hackathon night with MuleRun and an innovation
  hackathon for Toronto Tech Week, with teams formed on the night and demos
  at the end.
- **Maker Mondays.** Short online demo nights between the in-person events,
  so the community doesn't go quiet for a month.

## How I ran it

The unglamorous way. Found venues. Invited people, one at a time at first.
Built the itinerary for each night so a first-timer knew what would happen
and a regular had something new. Bought the signage. Ordered the pizza. Ran
the event, then did it again a few weeks later.

Then I built AI into the parts that don't scale by hand. An AI intake form
reads what each person is working on and forms hackathon teams around shared
interests, so teams are off building within minutes instead of standing
around. A matching tool on [makerslounge.ca](https://www.makerslounge.ca)
suggests who each attendee should meet. And every registration and check-in
syncs into my own agent, so I know who keeps coming back and what the last
night was like before I plan the next one.

## The numbers

Every event runs on Luma, so the whole funnel is on record. Registrations
are approved by hand to keep each room the right mix of people.

| Format | Events | Approved | Checked in | Show-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Workshops | 2 | 103 | 82 | 80% |
| Meetups | 7 | 552 | 192 | 35% |
| Hackathons | 2 | 218 | 46 | 21% |
| Online sessions | 9 | 167 | – | – |

In-person, that's 320 check-ins from 263 different people. 47 of them came
to two or more events, and the community around the events has grown to
1,500 builders in Toronto. What it produces is connections, the kind that
turn into co-founders, hires, customers and friends. The most common thing
people say is that it's a very welcoming room, which is the whole point. A
room where a beginner can show a half-finished thing without flinching is a
room where the good ideas come out.

## What the numbers taught me

Workshops win. A concrete outcome ("you'll leave with an agent running") and
a smaller room get four out of five people through the door. Hackathons leak:
approval costs nothing, so a big approved list shrinks to a fifth on the
night. So I judge a format by the people who actually show up and follow up,
not by signups, and I'd put the next dollar into workshops.

Some meetup nights were also too loud or too busy, and the people who came
hoping for individual attention didn't always get it. The fix is fewer people
per room and more hosts who know whose work to ask about, not bigger venues.
